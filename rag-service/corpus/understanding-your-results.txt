Understanding your screening results

WHAT YOUR RESULT MEANS

Your screening result describes a pattern in one piece of your writing. It is not a
diagnosis. It is not a label for you as a person. It describes what happened in that
sample, on that day.

A different piece of writing - written when you were less tired, or on a topic you knew
better - could show a different pattern. Treat the result as a starting point for
practice, not as a verdict.

WHY YOU MIGHT NOT HAVE BEEN GIVEN A PATTERN

The screener needs at least four spelling errors it can analyse before it will name a
pattern. Below that there is not enough evidence, so it says nothing rather than guessing.

Getting no pattern is not the same as having a problem, and it is not the same as being
told everything is fine. It usually means the sample was too short, or too accurate, to
draw anything from.

WHY YOUR RESULT MIGHT SAY MIXED

Sometimes two patterns come out almost equally strong. When they sit within a small margin
of each other, the screener reports both rather than picking a winner.

Mixed results are normal. Most writers show more than one kind of error.

THE KINDS OF ERROR THE SCREENER LOOKS FOR

Sounds are lost or changed.
The spelling does not represent the sounds of the word you meant. A sound is dropped,
added, or swapped for an unrelated one, so the word cannot be read back correctly.
Examples: "sret" for "street", where a cluster of consonants is reduced. "lettr" for
"letter", where a vowel is missing from a syllable. "famly" for "family", where a whole
syllable has gone.

Sounds right, spelled wrong.
Read your spelling aloud and it gives the right word, but it breaks an English spelling
convention. Examples: "enuf" for "enough". "wisle" for "whistle", where a silent letter is
missing. "skool" for "school", where the right sound is written with the wrong vowel team.
This points at the stored picture of the word rather than at how you hear it.

The root is right, the ending is wrong.
The main part of the word is spelled correctly and the error sits on the prefix or the
suffix. Sometimes the ending is left off, as in "walk" for "walked". Sometimes the wrong
ending is used, as in "walking" for "walked". Sometimes the ending is attached without the
spelling change English requires, giving "runing" instead of "running", or "happyness"
instead of "happiness". Sometimes the ending is spelled by sound, giving "walkt" for
"walked".

Letters reversed or swapped.
Letters that are mirror images of each other get exchanged, or two neighbouring letters
swap places. "gril" for "girl" is a swap. The pairs most often exchanged are b and d, p and
q, m and w, and n and u. In writers under about seven this is a normal stage of
development, so on its own it carries little weight. It matters when it appears alongside
other patterns.

The right sound, the wrong word.
A real word that sounds like the one you meant is used instead of it - their and there, to
and too. A spell-checker cannot catch these, because nothing is actually misspelled. They
depend on which word form you have stored, not on how you hear it.

Words joined or split.
Two words are run together, as in "alot", or one word is broken in half, as in "to gether".
This shows an unsettled sense of where one word ends and the next begins.

Word choice and agreement.
Sometimes a different real word is used, changing the grammar rather than the spelling.
This is reported for completeness. It does not feed into your spelling pattern.

WHAT COMES NEXT

Each pattern has its own guide, with practice that suits it. If you were given a pattern,
start with that guide. If you were not given one, any of them is a reasonable place to
read.
