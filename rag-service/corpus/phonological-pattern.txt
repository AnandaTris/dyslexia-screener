The phonological pattern

WHAT THIS PATTERN MEANS

Your errors mostly did not keep the sounds of the word you meant. When the spelling is read
back, it does not give the target word.

This is the signature associated with difficulty holding the map between sounds and
letters. It is about sound processing. It is not about intelligence, and it is not about
effort.

WHAT IT LOOKS LIKE IN WRITING

Consonant clusters get reduced. "street" becomes "sret" - the str cluster loses a sound.

Vowels drop out of syllables. "letter" becomes "lettr".

Whole syllables go missing. "family" becomes "famly".

Sounds get replaced by unrelated sounds, so the written word cannot be read back as the one
you meant.

Neighbouring letters may also swap places, as in "gril" for "girl". In this screener a swap
of that kind counts towards this pattern.

WHAT HELPS

The practice that suits this pattern is structured, explicit phonics. Work on the sounds
first and the letters second.

Phoneme segmentation. Take a spoken word and break it into its separate sounds. Say
"street" as s-t-r-ee-t. Count the sounds on your fingers before you write anything down.

Blending. The reverse of segmentation. Hear separate sounds and push them together into a
word. Start with three sounds, then four, then move to clusters.

Grapheme-phoneme correspondence drills. Work on one sound-to-letter mapping at a time until
it is automatic, rather than learning whole words by sight.

Multisensory work on consonant clusters. Say the sounds, tap them out, build them with
letter tiles, then write them. Using more than one sense at once gives the mapping more
than one route to stick.

Vowels inside syllables. Take a word, break it into syllables, and check that every
syllable has a vowel actually written in it. This catches the "lettr" error directly.

HOW TO PRACTISE

Work in short sessions, often, rather than long sessions rarely.

Say every word aloud before you write it.

When you get a word wrong, do not just correct the letters. Say the word slowly, break it
into its sounds, then write it again from the sounds. Correcting the spelling without
rebuilding the sounds practises the wrong thing.
